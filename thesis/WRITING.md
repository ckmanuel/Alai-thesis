# Thesis writing rules (binding)

These rules govern how thesis prose is written and revised in this repo. They apply to the working manuscript and any new thesis text.

If chat instructions and this file conflict on prose, **this file wins**. Layout conflicts are settled by `thesis/FORMATTING.md`.

---

## Priority order (binding)

When rules conflict, apply them in this order. Higher wins.

1. **Verb Tense Matrix** (`thesis/Thesis_Verb_Tense_Matrix.pdf`)
   - Use the FINAL DEFENSE column for the completed thesis.
   - Background / literature: present for current facts and ongoing situations; present perfect for trends that continue to now.
   - Statement of the problem and objectives: present (*aims*, *examines*, *seeks*).
   - Methods (completed study): past for what was done.
   - Results: past for what the analysis found; present for what the data show as enduring patterns when the matrix allows.
   - Discussion / conclusion: mix as the matrix specifies; keep claims tied to this corpus in past or present as appropriate, and keep general scholarly statements in present.

2. **Punctuation constraints**
   - Default to periods and fresh sentences.
   - Do **not** use em dashes (—) or semicolons (;) in thesis prose.
   - If a long sentence needs a break, split it. Prefer a full stop or one short subordinate clause.

3. **User preferences and academic register** (see also root `preferences.md`)
   - Philippine English spelling and educated written register.
   - Clear undergraduate academic English (roughly CEFR B1–B2 base, raised where the discipline requires precise terms).
   - Accuracy first, then clarity, then voice, then formatting.
   - Neutral wording: avoid informal, pathologizing, or deficit-framed labels.

## Tone and voice for this thesis

Write as a careful BAELS undergraduate researcher reporting a completed discourse analysis.

- Direct and plain. Name what the CTA phrases do, what the frameworks classify, and what the counts show.
- Prefer active voice where it fits (“The analysis identified…”, “Olay used…”). Passive is fine for methods and for focusing on the data (“Twenty-two phrases were classified as logos”).
- Short to medium sentences. One main idea per paragraph when possible.
- No purple prose, no filler openings (“In today’s digital age…”, “Since the dawn of social media…”).
- No manufactured drama. State the gap and the findings without intensifiers that the data do not support.
- Gender-neutral language unless the advertisement text itself is gendered and is being quoted or described.

## Background of the Study cadence

When revising or extending the BOTS, prefer this pattern (adapted from the PSAU BAELS hedge-thesis model used in the related memory repos):

1. A short opening that names the real situation or tension (not a definition dump).
2. Immediate fold-in of specific findings from real papers (author, year, what they actually found).
3. Clear gap stated in plain terms.
4. Study purpose paragraph that maps directly onto the research questions.
5. Citations only for claims the cited work supports. No filler citations.

---

## Claims and evidence

- Every frequency, percentage, and classification claim must match the tables and appendix coding.
- “Dominant” and similar words need a clear numerical or categorical basis.
- When comparing with other studies, state the difference in materials or unit of analysis; do not claim platform or culture effects the design cannot support.
- Keep the limit clear: this study explains the construction of persuasive meaning in the selected captions; it does not measure purchase or effectiveness.

---

## Framework language

- Aristotle: ethos (credibility), pathos (emotion), logos (reason / product information). Use the operational definitions already in Definition of Terms.
- Directives: command, order, request, suggestion per Yule (1996) as applied by Anggraini and Afriana (2023). Use those operational criteria; do not invent new subtype labels.
- CTA phrase is the primary unit of analysis; the full caption is context for rhetorical appeal.

---

## What not to do

- Do not insert em dashes or semicolons.
- Do not invent citations, frequencies, or subtype counts.
- Do not rewrite the title page structure or appendices’ coding colors unless asked.
- Do not add “In conclusion,” “It is worth noting that,” or similar empty frames.
- Do not soften a clear gap or a clear finding into vague hedging that the data do not require.
